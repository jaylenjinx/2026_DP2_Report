\section*{Q1: State-Space Model \hfill (40 marks)}
\subsection*{Q1.1) System Modelling}

\textbf{Definitions and assumptions.}
The QUBE-Servo 2 rotary pendulum comprises a motor-driven horizontal arm
and a pendulum hinged at its tip. Encoders measure the two angles
\cite{qube_manual}. Let $\theta$ denote arm angle and $\alpha$ pendulum
angle from the upward vertical, with $\alpha=0$ upright. Use radians.
Let $\boldsymbol e_r$ point along the arm, $\boldsymbol e_t$ point in the
direction of increasing $\theta$ and $\boldsymbol e_z$ point upward.
Positive $\alpha$ tilts the pendulum towards $-\boldsymbol e_t$.
This explicitly fixes the coupling signs.

The arm and pendulum are approximated as rigid uniform slender rods.
The pendulum centre of mass is at $l_p=L_p/2$. Viscous damping represents
friction. Motor inductance, structural flexibility, nonlinear friction
and pendulum longitudinal-axis inertia are neglected. The motor is direct
drive. These are nominal modelling assumptions, not measurements of
the particular laboratory unit.
\begin{equation}
x=\begin{bmatrix}\theta&\alpha&\dot\theta&\dot\alpha\end{bmatrix}^{T},
\qquad u=V_m,\qquad y=\begin{bmatrix}\theta&\alpha\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\end{equation}
Thus there are $n=4$ states, $m=1$ input and $p=2$ outputs. Velocities
are states even though only the angles are directly measured.

\textbf{Motor electrical dynamics.}
Kirchhoff's voltage law and the motor relations give
\begin{align}
V_m&=R_mi_m+L_m\frac{di_m}{dt}+e_b,\\
e_b&=k_m\dot\theta,\qquad \tau_m=k_ti_m.
\end{align}
Neglecting inductance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:motor}
i_m=\frac{V_m-k_m\dot\theta}{R_m},\qquad
\boxed{\tau_m=\frac{k_t}{R_m}V_m-\frac{k_tk_m}{R_m}\dot\theta.}
\end{equation}
Voltage generates torque and back emf reduces the current as speed rises.

\textbf{Inertias and energy.}
For a uniform rod, $dm=(m/L)\,dr$. The relevant inertias are
\begin{align}
J_{\mathrm{arm}}&=\int_0^{L_r}r^2\frac{m_r}{L_r}\,dr
=\frac13m_rL_r^2,\qquad J_r=J_{\mathrm{arm}}+J_m+J_h,\\
J_p&=\int_{-L_p/2}^{L_p/2}r^2\frac{m_p}{L_p}\,dr
=\frac1{12}m_pL_p^2.
\end{align}
Here $J_r$ includes the arm, rotor and hub. The arm rod approximation
excludes the separately added rotor and hub. No inertia-disc module is
included. $J_p$ is about the pendulum centre of mass; the hinge inertia is
$J_p+m_pl_p^2=\frac13m_pL_p^2$ by the parallel-axis theorem.

The pendulum centre-of-mass position is
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol r_p=L_r\boldsymbol e_r-l_p\sin\alpha\,\boldsymbol e_t
+l_p\cos\alpha\,\boldsymbol e_z.
\end{equation}
Using $\dot{\boldsymbol e}_r=\dot\theta\boldsymbol e_t$ and
$\dot{\boldsymbol e}_t=-\dot\theta\boldsymbol e_r$ gives
\begin{align}
\dot{\boldsymbol r}_p&=l_p\sin\alpha\,\dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_r
+(L_r\dot\theta-l_p\cos\alpha\,\dot\alpha)\boldsymbol e_t
-l_p\sin\alpha\,\dot\alpha\,\boldsymbol e_z,\\
\|\dot{\boldsymbol r}_p\|^2&=(L_r^2+l_p^2\sin^2\alpha)\dot\theta^2
+l_p^2\dot\alpha^2-2L_rl_p\cos\alpha\,\dot\theta\dot\alpha.
\end{align}
Neglecting longitudinal-axis inertia, pendulum rotation about its centre
of mass contributes
\[\frac12J_p(\dot\alpha^2+\sin^2\alpha\,\dot\theta^2).\]
Therefore the total kinetic and potential energies are
\begin{align}
T&=\frac12J_r\dot\theta^2+\frac12m_p\|\dot{\boldsymbol r}_p\|^2
+\frac12J_p(\dot\alpha^2+\sin^2\alpha\,\dot\theta^2),\\
U&=m_pgl_p\cos\alpha.
\end{align}
Potential energy is referenced to the hinge height and is maximal upright.

Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constants}
a=J_r+m_pL_r^2,\quad b=J_p+m_pl_p^2,\quad
c=m_pL_rl_p,\quad h=m_pgl_p.
\end{equation}
The Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrangian}
\mathcal L=T-U=
\frac12(a+b\sin^2\alpha)\dot\theta^2+\frac12b\dot\alpha^2
-c\cos\alpha\,\dot\theta\dot\alpha-h\cos\alpha.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Euler--Lagrange equations.}
For $q\in\{\theta,\alpha\}$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q}\right)
-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q}=Q_q,\qquad
Q_\theta=\tau_m-D_r\dot\theta,\quad Q_\alpha=-D_p\dot\alpha.
\end{equation}
The arm derivatives are
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\theta}&=0,\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta}
&=(a+b\sin^2\alpha)\dot\theta-c\cos\alpha\,\dot\alpha,\\
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta}\right)
&=(a+b\sin^2\alpha)\ddot\theta
+2b\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\,\dot\alpha\dot\theta
-c\cos\alpha\,\ddot\alpha+c\sin\alpha\,\dot\alpha^2.
\end{align}
The pendulum derivatives are
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\alpha}
&=b\dot\alpha-c\cos\alpha\,\dot\theta,\\
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\alpha}\right)
&=b\ddot\alpha-c\cos\alpha\,\ddot\theta
+c\sin\alpha\,\dot\alpha\dot\theta,\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\alpha}
&=b\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\,\dot\theta^2
+c\sin\alpha\,\dot\theta\dot\alpha+h\sin\alpha.
\end{align}
Define $k_v=k_t/R_m$ and $d=D_r+k_tk_m/R_m$. The resulting
nonlinear mechanical equations, including motor torque, are
\begin{samepage}
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:eom}
\begin{align}
(a+b\sin^2\alpha)\ddot\theta-c\cos\alpha\,\ddot\alpha
+2b\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\,\dot\alpha\dot\theta
+c\sin\alpha\,\dot\alpha^2&=k_vu-d\dot\theta,\\
-c\cos\alpha\,\ddot\theta+b\ddot\alpha
-b\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\,\dot\theta^2-h\sin\alpha&=-D_p\dot\alpha.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\end{samepage}
Fixing the arm and neglecting friction gives $b\ddot\alpha=h\sin\alpha$.
A small positive tilt therefore accelerates away from upright, which
provides a physical check on the gravity sign.

\textbf{Nonlinear state-space representation.}
Define the torque expressions
\begin{align}
r_1(x,u)&=k_vu-dx_3-2b\sin x_2\cos x_2\,x_4x_3-c\sin x_2\,x_4^2,\\
r_2(x,u)&=b\sin x_2\cos x_2\,x_3^2+h\sin x_2-D_px_4.
\end{align}
The equations of motion become
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
a+b\sin^2\alpha&-c\cos\alpha\\-c\cos\alpha&b
\end{bmatrix}}_{M(\alpha)}
\begin{bmatrix}\ddot\theta\\\ddot\alpha\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}r_1\\r_2\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The mass-matrix determinant and inverse are
\begin{align}
\Delta(\alpha)&=b(a+b\sin^2\alpha)-c^2\cos^2\alpha,\\
M^{-1}(\alpha)&=\frac1{\Delta(\alpha)}
\begin{bmatrix}b&c\cos\alpha\\c\cos\alpha&a+b\sin^2\alpha\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonlinear_ss}
\boxed{\dot x=f(x,u)=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_3\\x_4\\
(br_1+c\cos x_2\,r_2)/\Delta(x_2)\\
(c\cos x_2\,r_1+(a+b\sin^2x_2)r_2)/\Delta(x_2)
\end{bmatrix}.}
\end{equation}
For the parameters in Q1.2, $ab-c^2>0$ and
$\Delta(\alpha)=(ab-c^2)+(b^2+c^2)\sin^2\alpha>0$, so this idealised
model is nonsingular for every pendulum angle.

\textbf{Equilibrium and Jacobian linearisation.}
An upright stationary equilibrium is
\begin{equation}
x_e=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_e&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}^{T},\qquad u_e=0.
\end{equation}
Any fixed $\theta_e$ is an ideal equilibrium, since no restoring torque
depends on arm angle. Choose $\theta_e=0$ for the numerical model.
Define $\delta x=x-x_e$, $\delta u=u-u_e$ and $\delta y=y-y_e$.
The first-order Taylor expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ct_ss}
\delta\dot x=A_c\delta x+B_c\delta u,\qquad
\delta y=C_c\delta x+D_c\delta u,
\end{equation}
with Jacobians
\begin{equation}
A_c=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_e,\qquad
B_c=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right|_e.
\end{equation}
At equilibrium, $r_1=r_2=0$ and $\Delta_0=ab-c^2$. Also,
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\partial(r_1,r_2)}{\partial x}\right|_e
=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-d&0\\0&h&0&-D_p\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\left.\frac{\partial(r_1,r_2)}{\partial u}\right|_e
=\begin{bmatrix}k_v\\0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Derivatives of $M^{-1}$ multiply the zero equilibrium torque vector.
Multiplying the above derivatives by
$M^{-1}(0)=\Delta_0^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}b&c\\c&a\end{bmatrix}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ac_symbolic}
\boxed{A_c=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&1\\
0&ch/\Delta_0&-bd/\Delta_0&-cD_p/\Delta_0\\
0&ah/\Delta_0&-cd/\Delta_0&-aD_p/\Delta_0
\end{bmatrix},\quad
B_c=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\bk_v/\Delta_0\\ck_v/\Delta_0\end{bmatrix}.}
\end{equation}
Since the outputs are the two angles with no direct voltage feedthrough,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CD}
\boxed{C_c=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&1&0&0\end{bmatrix},
\qquad D_c=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}.}
\end{equation}
This linearisation describes small deviations about upright; large-angle
motion requires the nonlinear model in~\eqref{eq:nonlinear_ss}.

\subsection*{Q1.3) System Model Discretisation}

Choose the sampling time
\begin{equation}
\boxed{T_s=0.001~\mathrm s,\qquad f_s=1/T_s=1000~\mathrm{Hz}.}
\end{equation}
This matches the value in the supplied ControLab template's
\texttt{Hardware\_settings.m}~\cite{hardware_template}. It is also short
relative to the mechanical dynamics, since
$T_s\max_i|\lambda_i(A_c)|\simeq0.01763\ll1$ for this model. Hardware execution timing
and achieved closed-loop performance must still be checked during
implementation.

Zero-order hold (ZOH) assumes that motor voltage is held constant between
controller updates. The exact discretisation of the linearised model
under this assumption is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zoh}
A=e^{A_cT_s},\qquad
B=\int_0^{T_s}e^{A_c\tau}B_c\,d\tau,\qquad C=C_c,\quad D=D_c.
\end{equation}
The MATLAB function \texttt{c2d} evaluates these expressions without
requiring an inverse of $A_c$. This matters because the arm-position mode
makes $A_c$ singular.
The discrete-time model is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:dt_ss}
\begin{align}
\delta x[k+1]&=A\delta x[k]+B\delta u[k],\\
\delta y[k]&=C\delta x[k]+D\delta u[k].
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
At the chosen zero equilibrium the deviation variables equal the
original variables. The calculated matrices are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A_discrete}
A\simeq
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2.70127657\!\times\!10^{-5}&9.96918625\!\times\!10^{-4}&-8.80391044\!\times\!10^{-7}\\
0&1.00008362958&-3.04176177\!\times\!10^{-6}&9.97274363\!\times\!10^{-4}\\
0&0.05392103923&0.99384536883&-0.00174835485\\
0&0.16705290505&-0.00607175726&0.99458335877
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B\simeq\begin{bmatrix}
9.00986699\times10^{-6}\\8.89404027\times10^{-6}\\
0.0179959975588\\0.0177536761881
\end{bmatrix},\quad
C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&1&0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad
D=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The dimensions are $A\in\mathbb R^{4\times4}$,
$B\in\mathbb R^{4\times1}$, $C\in\mathbb R^{2\times4}$ and
$D\in\mathbb R^{2\times1}$. Displayed results are rounded;
full precision must be retained in MATLAB for controller and observer
design.

lp = Lp/2;
Jr = mr*Lr^2/3 + Jm + Jh;
Jp = mp*Lp^2/12;
a = Jr + mp*Lr^2;
b = Jp + mp*lp^2;
c = mp*Lr*lp;
h = mp*g*lp;
d = Dr + kt*km/Rm;
kv = kt/Rm;
Delta0 = a*b - c^2;

%% Continuous model: [theta; alpha; theta_dot; alpha_dot]
Ac = [0, 0, 1, 0;
      0, 0, 0, 1;
      0, c*h/Delta0, -b*d/Delta0, -c*Dp/Delta0;
      0, a*h/Delta0, -c*d/Delta0, -a*Dp/Delta0];
Bc = [0; 0; b*kv/Delta0; c*kv/Delta0];
Cc = [1 0 0 0; 0 1 0 0];
Dc = zeros(2,1);
sys_ct = ss(Ac,Bc,Cc,Dc);

%% Exact zero-order-hold discretisation
ts = 0.001;
sys_dt = c2d(sys_ct,ts,'zoh');
format long g
Ac, Bc, Cc, Dc
A = sys_dt.A
B = sys_dt.B
C = sys_dt.C
D = sys_dt.D
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Q2.1) Controllability}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
For the four-state continuous-time model obtained in Q1, the controllability matrix is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        B_c & A_cB_c & A_c^2B_c & A_c^3B_c
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Using the full-precision matrices in MATLAB gives
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C} \approx
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 18.06739 & -143.52761 & 2228.45594 \\
0 & 17.85731 & -208.92687 & 5024.20650 \\
18.06739 & -143.52761 & 2228.45594 & -34072.92634 \\
17.85731 & -208.92687 & 5024.20650 & -76378.80248
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The rank is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{C}) = 4 = n.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the nominal continuous-time system is completely controllable. Although the system has only one motor-voltage input, the coupling between the rotary arm and pendulum allows this input to influence all four states.

The controllability matrix of the discrete-time pair $(A,B)$ was also checked and has rank four at the selected sampling period $T_s=0.001\,\mathrm{s}$. Consequently, all four discrete-time closed-loop poles can be assigned using state feedback.

The MATLAB calculations are:
\begin{verbatim}
CMc = ctrb(Ac,Bc);
CMd = ctrb(A,B);
rank(CMc)
rank(CMd)
\end{verbatim}

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Q2.2) State and Input References (Equilibrium Point)}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
Given the desired output reference $y^\star$, the steady-state references
$x^\star$ and $u^\star$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} A - I & B\\ C & D\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} x^\star\\ u^\star\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ y^\star\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The desired output is a specified arm angle while maintaining the pendulum upright:
\begin{equation}
    y^\star =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \theta_{\mathrm{ref}} \\ 0
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

At a stationary equilibrium, both angular velocities and accelerations are zero. With $\alpha=0$, substitution into the nonlinear equations of motion gives zero required motor voltage. Hence,
\begin{equation}
    x^\star =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \theta_{\mathrm{ref}} \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    u^\star = 0.
\end{equation}

These references satisfy
\begin{equation}
    (A-I)x^\star + Bu^\star = 0,
    \qquad
    Cx^\star + Du^\star = y^\star.
\end{equation}

For the simulation, a $5^\circ$ arm-angle step is applied at
$t=1\,\mathrm{s}$. The reference is therefore
\begin{equation}
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)=
\begin{cases}
0, & t<1\,\mathrm{s},\\
5\pi/180, & t\geq1\,\mathrm{s}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

After the step,
\begin{equation}
    x^\star =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.08726646 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    u^\star=0\,\mathrm{V}.
\end{equation}

The pendulum reference remains zero throughout. The two output references cannot be selected independently for a stationary upright equilibrium: an arbitrary nonzero pendulum tilt cannot be maintained with the arm stationary.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Q2.3) Closed-Loop Eigenvalues}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
% Project requirements: overshoot < 15 %, settling time < 5 s,
% steady-state accuracy < 50 (check units on Canvas).

The control requirements are an overshoot below $15\,\%$ and a settling time
below $5~\mathrm{s}$. The damping ratio and natural frequency follow from
\begin{equation}
  \zeta = \frac{-\ln(OS/100)}{\sqrt{\pi^2+\ln^2(OS/100)}},\qquad
  \omega_n = \frac{4}{\zeta\,t_s},
\end{equation}
and the discrete-time eigenvalues from $z_i = e^{s_i T_s}$.

A $2\%$ settling band is used when evaluating the responses.

For an ideal second-order system, the percentage overshoot is
\begin{equation}
    OS = 100
    \exp\left(
        \frac{-\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}
    \right).
\end{equation}

An overshoot of $15\%$ corresponds to a damping ratio of approximately $\zeta=0.517$. To provide design margin, choose
\begin{equation}
    \zeta=0.6,
    \qquad
    \omega_n=2.5\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
\end{equation}

The dominant continuous-time poles are
\begin{equation}
    s_{1,2}
    =-\zeta\omega_n
    \pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}
    =-1.5\pm j2.
\end{equation}

The corresponding second-order estimates are
\begin{equation}
    OS \approx 9.478\%,
    \qquad
    t_{\mathrm{settle}}
    \approx\frac{4}{\zeta\omega_n}
    =2.667\,\mathrm{s}.
\end{equation}

The remaining two poles are selected at $-6$ and $-8\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, giving faster decay rates than the dominant pair. The complete continuous-time pole selection is
\begin{equation}
    p_s=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -1.5+j2 & -1.5-j2 & -6 & -8
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Using $T_s=0.001\,\mathrm{s}$, the discrete-time poles are obtained from $z_i=e^{s_iT_s}$:
\begin{align}
    z_{1,2}
    &\approx 0.9984991274 \pm j0.0019970009,\\
    z_3 &\approx 0.9940179641,\\
    z_4 &\approx 0.9920319148.
\end{align}

All selected discrete-time poles lie strictly inside the unit circle. The second-order estimates guide the initial design; the performance of the complete fourth-order system is evaluated using the Simulink responses.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Q2.4) State-Feedback Gain}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
For the control law
The discrete-time state-feedback control law is
\begin{equation}
    u(k)=-F\bigl(x(k)-x^\star(k)\bigr)+u^\star(k).
\end{equation}

The feedback gain is calculated using the full-precision discrete-time matrices and the selected poles:
\begin{verbatim}
pz = exp(ps*ts);
F = place(A,B,pz);
eig(A-B*F)
\end{verbatim}

The resulting gain is
\begin{equation}
    F \approx
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -0.14517718 &
        15.38254012 &
        -0.45888105 &
        0.76529169
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Since there is one control input and four states,
$F\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times4}$.

MATLAB gives the achieved eigenvalues
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(A-BF)\approx
    \left\{
    \begin{array}{l}
        0.9920319148,\\
        0.9940179641,\\
        0.9984991274+j0.0019970009,\\
        0.9984991274-j0.0019970009
    \end{array}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

These agree with the desired discrete-time poles to numerical precision. Therefore, the nominal unsaturated discrete-time closed loop is asymptotically stable. The effects of input limits and nonlinear dynamics are assessed through simulation.

\section*{Q3: Luenberger Observer Design \hfill (20 marks)}
%=======================================================================

The controller of Q2 needs the full state
$x=[\theta\;\;\alpha\;\;\dot\theta\;\;\dot\alpha]^{T}$, but the QUBE-Servo 2
measures only the two angles through its encoders, $y=C_cx=[\theta\;\;\alpha]^{T}$.
The angular velocities could be obtained by differencing the encoder signals.
At $T_s=0.001~\mathrm s$, however, a single encoder count
($2\pi/2048=0.00307~\mathrm{rad}=0.176^\circ$~\cite{qube_manual}) would appear
as a velocity step of $176^\circ/\mathrm s$. Instead, a discrete-time
Luenberger observer reconstructs the full state from the measured angles and
the applied voltage, using the model of Q1.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Q3.1) Observability}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
With $n=4$ states and $p=2$ outputs, the observability matrix of the
continuous-time model~\eqref{eq:ct_ss} is the $np\times n=8\times4$ matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obsv}
  \mathcal{O}=
  \begin{bmatrix} C_c\\ C_cA_c\\ C_cA_c^{2}\\ C_cA_c^{3}\end{bmatrix}
  \approx
  \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
     1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
     0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ \hline
     0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\
     0 & 0 & 0 & 1\\ \hline
     0 & 54.236 & -6.179 & -1.786\\
     0 & 167.675 & -6.107 & -5.521\\ \hline
     0 & -634.55 & 49.086 & 75.128\\
     0 & -1256.9 & 71.453 & 209.06
  \end{array}\right],
\end{equation}
computed with the full-precision matrices of Q1:
\begin{verbatim}
OM = obsv(Ac,Cc);
rank(OM)
rank(obsv(A,C))
\end{verbatim}

The first four rows of~\eqref{eq:obsv} form the $4\times4$ identity matrix.
This is because $C_c$ selects the two angles and $C_cA_c$ selects the two
angular velocities (the first two rows of $A_c$ in~\eqref{eq:Ac_symbolic} are
$[0\;0\;1\;0]$ and $[0\;0\;0\;1]$). Therefore
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{O})=4=n,
\end{equation}
and the continuous-time system is \textbf{completely observable}.

From a control viewpoint, the two unmeasured states are the time derivatives
of the two measured outputs. Any initial velocity therefore leaves a
distinguishable trace in $y(t)$ almost immediately, so the full state can be
reconstructed from a short window of input--output data. Unlike
controllability in Q2.1, which relies on the arm--pendulum coupling, here every
state is reached through the kinematic chain from angle to velocity alone. The
discrete-time pair $(A,C)$ also has rank~4. Sampling cannot destroy
observability here because $T_s$ is much shorter than every open-loop time
constant (open-loop eigenvalues $0$, $9.95$, $-4.02$ and
$-17.63~\mathrm{s^{-1}}$).

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Q3.2) Observer Eigenvalues}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
By the separation principle~\cite{aguilera2026}, the eigenvalues of the
observer-based closed loop are the union of the state-feedback eigenvalues,
$\lambda(A-BF)$, and the observer eigenvalues, $\lambda(A-LC)$. The two designs
can therefore be carried out independently. The estimation error should decay
clearly faster than the regulated response, so that by the time the
controller's dynamics evolve, it is effectively acting on the true state.

The state-feedback poles from Q2.3 are
$p_s=\{-1.5\pm j2,\;-6,\;-8\}~\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. A common design rule places
the observer poles two to six times faster than the fastest controller pole.
Here a factor of five is used, with distinct real values:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obs_poles_s}
  p_{o,s}=\{-40,\;-45,\;-50,\;-55\}~\mathrm{s^{-1}}.
\end{equation}
The reasons for this choice are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The slowest observer mode has time constant $1/40=25~\mathrm{ms}$, so
        the estimation error is reduced by a factor $e^{-4}\approx2\%$ within
        about $0.1~\mathrm s$. That is $27$ times faster than the
        $1/1.5=0.67~\mathrm s$ time constant of the dominant controller pair,
        and far shorter than the $2.7~\mathrm s$ closed-loop settling time.
  \item Real poles give a non-oscillatory error decay. They are distinct
        because \texttt{place} only allows a pole multiplicity of up to
        $\operatorname{rank}(C)=2$.
  \item The poles are not pushed further left. A faster observer needs a larger
        gain $L$, which passes more encoder quantisation noise into $\hat x$ and
        increases the initial ``peaking'' of the velocity estimates (Q3.4).
        With $|p_{o,s}|T_s\le0.055$, each observer time constant still spans
        at least $18$ samples, so the discrete design is well resolved.
\end{itemize}

Mapping these poles to the $z$-plane with $z_i=e^{s_iT_s}$ and
$T_s=0.001~\mathrm s$ (MATLAB: \texttt{po\_z = exp(po\_s*ts)}) gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obs_poles_z}
  p_{o,z}\approx\{0.96079,\;0.95600,\;0.95123,\;0.94649\}.
\end{equation}
All lie on the positive real axis, strictly inside the unit circle and closer
to the origin than the controller poles of Q2.3,
$\{0.99850\pm j0.00200,\;0.99402,\;0.99203\}$. This confirms in the
$z$-domain that the estimation error decays faster than the closed-loop
response.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Q3.3) Observer Gain}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
The discrete-time Luenberger observer is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:observer}
  \hat{x}(k+1)=A\hat{x}(k)+Bu(k)+L\bigl(y(k)-\hat{y}(k)\bigr),
  \qquad \hat{y}(k)=C\hat{x}(k).
\end{equation}
It is a copy of the discrete model~\eqref{eq:dt_ss}, driven by the same input
and corrected by the output innovation $y(k)-\hat y(k)$. Subtracting it from
the plant model, the estimation error $e(k)=x(k)-\hat{x}(k)$ evolves as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:err_dyn}
  e(k+1)=(A-LC)\,e(k),
\end{equation}
independently of $u(k)$ and of the reference. Because
$\lambda(A-LC)=\lambda(A^{T}-C^{T}L^{T})$, computing $L$ is the dual of a
state-feedback design for the pair $(A^{T},C^{T})$. That design is solvable
because $(A,C)$ is observable (Q3.1):
\begin{verbatim}
po_s = [-40 -45 -50 -55];
po_z = exp(po_s*ts);
Lo   = place(A',C',po_z)';
eig(A - Lo*C)
\end{verbatim}
The resulting $n\times p=4\times2$ observer gain is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L}
  L\approx
  \begin{bmatrix}
     0.087990 & -0.005869\\
    -0.011086 &  0.086021\\
     1.6806   & -0.25164\\
    -0.70415  &  1.8098
  \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and MATLAB returns
\begin{equation}
  \lambda(A-LC)\approx\{0.96079,\;0.95600,\;0.95123,\;0.94649\},
\end{equation}
which matches~\eqref{eq:obs_poles_z} to numerical precision. The observer
therefore has the desired eigenvalues, and the error dynamics~\eqref{eq:err_dyn}
are asymptotically stable.

The structure of $L$ has a clear physical reading. Column~1 weights the arm
innovation $\theta-\hat\theta$, and column~2 weights the pendulum innovation
$\alpha-\hat\alpha$. Each angle estimate is corrected mainly by its own
measurement, by about $0.088$ of the innovation per sample (diagonal of the
first two rows). Each velocity estimate is corrected by about
$1.7~\mathrm{(rad/s)/rad}$ per sample from its own angle innovation, which
acts as a model-based, filtered differentiator. One encoder count of
innovation therefore changes $\hat{\dot\theta}$ by only about
$1.68\times0.00307~\mathrm{rad/s}=0.30^\circ/\mathrm s$, compared with the
$176^\circ/\mathrm s$ jump of a raw finite difference. The off-diagonal terms
account for the arm--pendulum coupling in $A$.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Q3.4) Closed-Loop Implementation}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
The observer runs in the same MATLAB Function block as the state feedback of
Q2.5, sampled at $T_s=0.001~\mathrm s$, with \texttt{run\_observer = 1}. The
controller now feeds back the estimated state:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sfc_obs}
  u(k)=-F\bigl(\hat{x}(k)-x^\star(k)\bigr)+u^\star(k),
\end{equation}
where $F$, $x^\star$ and $u^\star=0$ are unchanged from Q2.2--Q2.4. The
relevant part of the block is:
\begin{lstlisting}
x_hat = x_hat_k;                      % estimate used at sample k
x_ref = [y_ref(1); 0; 0; 0];          % Q2.2
u_ref = 0;
u_k = -F*(x_hat_k - x_ref) + u_ref;   % SFC on the estimated state
u_k = min(max(u_k, -10), 10);         % +/-10 V amplifier limit
y_hat   = C*x_hat_k;                  % Luenberger observer (Q3.3)
x_hat_k = A*x_hat_k + B*u_k + Lo*(y_k - y_hat);
\end{lstlisting}
The observer is updated with the \emph{saturated} voltage that actually reaches
the motor. If it were fed the unsaturated command, its model input would differ
from the plant input whenever the amplifier limits, and $\hat x$ would drift.

The same controller and observer were applied to the linearised and nonlinear
Simulink plants of Q2.5. As Remark~5 asks, the plant starts at rest upright,
$x(0)=0$, while the observer is deliberately initialised with
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}(0)=\bigl[\,3^\circ\;\;-1^\circ\;\;0\;\;0\,\bigr]^{T}\neq x(0).
\end{equation}
In the observer tests the $5^\circ$ arm step is applied at $t=5~\mathrm s$
instead of $1~\mathrm s$. The reason is explained under ``Effect of the initial
estimation error'' below: this separates the start-up transient caused by
$\hat x(0)$ from the tracking test. The step time is set from
\texttt{main\_SFC.m} at run time, and the Q2.5 configuration is unchanged.
Signals were exported to the workspace and plotted with
\texttt{plot\_closed\_loop.m}. The script \texttt{run\_all\_cases.m}
regenerates every Q3 figure.

\textbf{Observer convergence.}
Figure~\ref{fig:q34_estimation} compares the true and estimated states of the
nonlinear model, with a zoom on the first $0.3~\mathrm s$.
Figure~\ref{fig:q34_error} shows the estimation error on a logarithmic scale.
The angle estimates converge onto the measured angles within about
$25~\mathrm{ms}$, and both angle errors stay below $0.01^\circ$ after
$153~\mathrm{ms}$ in both the linear and nonlinear cases. This agrees with
Q3.2, since reducing a $3^\circ$ error to $0.01^\circ$ takes
$\ln(300)\approx5.7$ time constants of the slowest observer mode, or about
$143~\mathrm{ms}$. After the first few samples, the error curves in
Figure~\ref{fig:q34_error} are straight lines on the logarithmic axis, which is
the exponential decay predicted by~\eqref{eq:err_dyn}. The final estimation
error is about $10^{-13}$, at the level of numerical round-off.

The zoom also shows the price of estimating unmeasured states. The velocity
estimates start with zero error, but the initial $3^\circ$ and $-1^\circ$
angle innovations pass through the velocity rows of $L$. They therefore
produce velocity-estimate errors that peak at $41.8^\circ/\mathrm s$
($\dot\theta$) and $28.5^\circ/\mathrm s$ ($\dot\alpha$) about
$20~\mathrm{ms}$ after start-up, before decaying with the rest of the error.
This transient ``peaking'' grows with observer speed, and it is the main reason
the observer poles were not placed further left in Q3.2.

\textbf{Effect of the initial estimation error.}
Figures~\ref{fig:q34_closed_loop_L} and~\ref{fig:q34_closed_loop_NL} show the
full closed-loop responses. Because $u(k)$ is computed from $\hat x(k)$, the
wrong initial estimate acts on the plant like an initial-condition disturbance.
The controller ``sees'' the pendulum $1^\circ$ from upright and responds with
$u(0)=0.276~\mathrm V$. The gain on the pendulum angle,
$F_\alpha=15.38~\mathrm{V/rad}$, is about $100$ times the gain on the arm
angle, $F_\theta=-0.145~\mathrm{V/rad}$. This is the correct design for an
inverted pendulum, but it means a small pendulum-estimate error commands a
large arm motion. Over the $\approx0.1~\mathrm s$ the estimate takes to
converge, the plant is pushed far enough that the arm swings to $18.3^\circ$
and the pendulum to $1.61^\circ$. The recovery is then governed by the
deliberately gentle dominant poles of Q2.3 ($\sigma=-1.5~\mathrm{s^{-1}}$) and
takes roughly $3~\mathrm s$. The peak voltage, $0.71~\mathrm V$, stays well
inside the $\pm10~\mathrm V$ limit, so the loop remains linear throughout.
By $t=5~\mathrm s$ the arm is back within $0.01^\circ$ of zero, which is why
the tracking step was moved there.

\textbf{Closed-loop performance.}
Figure~\ref{fig:q34_step_zoom} zooms in on the $5^\circ$ step, and
Table~\ref{tab:q34_metrics} compares it with the full-state feedback results of
Q2.5. Once the estimate has converged, $\hat x(k)\approx x(k)$ and, by the
separation principle, the response is governed only by $\lambda(A-BF)$. The
observer-based step response is accordingly indistinguishable from Q2.5:
$8.39\,\%$ overshoot against $8.37\,\%$, and $2.715~\mathrm s$ settling time
against $2.713~\mathrm s$. Both are well within the requirements of
$<15\,\%$ and $<5~\mathrm s$, with negligible steady-state error. The estimated
arm angle in Figure~\ref{fig:q34_step_zoom} lies on top of the true one during
the whole manoeuvre.

The small differences between the two rows of each pair are the last remnant
of the start-up transient, which has not fully decayed at $t=5~\mathrm s$. The
linear and nonlinear results agree to the precision shown, because the
pendulum never leaves $|\alpha|<1.7^\circ$. At that angle the approximation
$\sin\alpha\approx\alpha$ is accurate to about $0.01\,\%$, so the linearised
model on which both $F$ and $L$ are based describes the plant faithfully.

On the real QUBE-Servo 2, two practical points follow. First, the start-up
swing can be avoided by initialising $\hat\theta(0)$ and $\hat\alpha(0)$ from
the first encoder readings. Then only the velocities, which are zero at rest,
need to be estimated, and the initial innovation is close to zero. Second, the
simulated steady-state error is zero only because the model is exact.
Unmodelled Coulomb friction and parameter mismatch on the hardware would leave
an arm-angle offset that this proportional state feedback cannot remove
without integral action.
